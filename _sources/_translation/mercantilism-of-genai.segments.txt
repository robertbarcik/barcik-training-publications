#### 29 div class=dn-label
I might be wrong about all of this

#### 30 p
What follows is an <strong>interpretation</strong>, written in the hours after a single event, by one person watching from the outside. It is not reporting, not a forecast, and certainly not a leak of anyone's strategy. I have no inside knowledge of any lab or any government. I am pattern-matching an old idea onto a fast-moving present, and pattern-matching is exactly the activity that feels most like insight while being most prone to error.

#### 31 p
So take this the way you should take everything: with a sceptical mind. Where I state something that actually happened, I try to mark it <span class="tag tag-reported">reported</span>. Where I'm interpreting, inferring, or guessing, I try to mark it <span class="tag tag-speculation">interpretation</span>. The two are not the same and I don't want to blur them.

#### 32 p
The value here, if there is any, sits in the <strong>lens</strong> rather than in the predictions, of which I make almost none. If the lens helps you read the next headline more clearly than the last one, it has done its job, even if every specific guess I make turns out wrong.

#### 33 p
Two things to know before you start. This booklet was <strong>co-written with AI</strong>: the June edition with Claude (Opus 4.8), and the July update with Claude (Fable 5), the very model this booklet is about. Near the end, <a href="#coauthor" style="color:var(--risk-color)">each signs its own honest note</a> about what it thinks of what we made, including why you should be wary of it. #### 35 h2
The morning it went dark <br><span class="chapter-question">&ldquo;What just happened, and what do I make of it?&rdquo;</span>

#### 36 p class=lead
On the evening of June 12, 2026, at 5:21pm Eastern, Anthropic received a directive from the US government and switched off two of its models, Claude Mythos 5 and the safeguard-wrapped Claude Fable 5, for every user on Earth. <span class="tag tag-reported">reported</span> They had been generally available for three days.

#### 37 p
I had spent the previous day working with Fable. I woke up to find the collaborator behind glass. That's a small, personal thing, and I mention it only because the personal jolt is what sent me looking for a frame: the official explanation didn't fit the size of the action, and the mismatch nagged at me.

#### 38 p
Here is what the directive actually said, because the precise wording is the whole story. It ordered Anthropic to suspend access to those models <em>for any foreign national, whether inside or outside the United States</em>, reportedly including Anthropic's own foreign-national employees. <span class="tag tag-reported">reported</span> Notice the controlled variable: not <em>where you are</em>, but <em>what nationality you are</em>. And an account simply does not carry a verifiable nationality; an IP address is not a passport, a billing address is not a citizenship, and the person typing is not necessarily the person who signed up. Faced with a line drawn on the one attribute it cannot see, Anthropic had exactly one compliant move: pull the models for <em>everyone</em>, Americans included. The unverifiability of nationality didn't soften the order; it detonated it.

#### 39 p
The stated reason made the action stranger, not clearer. The government's concern, as Anthropic described it, was a possible &ldquo;jailbreak&rdquo;, and the jailbreak amounted to <em>asking the model to read a codebase and fix its software flaws.</em> <span class="tag tag-reported">reported</span> That is not an exploit; that is the model doing the job it was built for. Anthropic disagreed sharply in public, arguing that a &ldquo;narrow, non-universal&rdquo; finding shouldn't recall a model deployed to hundreds of millions, and that the same standard, applied across the industry, &ldquo;would essentially halt all new model deployments for all frontier model providers.&rdquo; <span class="tag tag-reported">reported</span>

#### 40 p
So we have an action of enormous scope justified by a reason that can't carry its weight. When the stated reason is too small for the deed, it is not paranoid to suspect that the reason is not the whole point. That suspicion is where this booklet starts.

#### 41 h3
This booklet is a lens, not a forecast

#### 42 p
I'm not going to tell you whether Mythos comes back in two months or never. I genuinely don't know, and anyone who claims to is selling something. What I want to offer instead is a <strong>way of reading</strong>: a structure that stays useful regardless of which way the specific event breaks. Because the morning it went dark, the question millions of people actually asked was not &ldquo;when does my model return&rdquo; but the older, vaguer one: <em>what is happening here, at a level bigger than this one product?</em>

#### 43 p
My answer is that we are watching an old idea come back wearing new clothes. The behavior of this entire industry (the recall, the export controls, the open-versus-closed weight games, the restrictions on chips, the way the world is sorting into blocs) becomes legible the moment you stop seeing AI as a <em>product</em> and start seeing it as the object of <strong>mercantilism</strong>: the doctrine that treats a strategic resource as something a nation accumulates, walls, and wields against its rivals. The next section explains the idea plainly. Everything after that is the idea applied.

#### 44 p class=credits
Primary source: Anthropic, &ldquo;Statement on the US government directive to suspend access to Fable 5 and Mythos 5&rdquo; &bull; corroborated by CNBC, 9to5Mac, The Week (June 12&ndash;13, 2026)

#### 45 div class=chapter-label
The Lens

#### 46 h2
What mercantilism is <br><span class="chapter-question">&ldquo;An instrument for reading the rest of this booklet&rdquo;</span>

#### 47 p
Bear with two paragraphs of the 1600s; they end in a table that makes every AI headline this year readable. Picture the world the idea ran. A handful of European powers (England, Spain, the Dutch Republic) measure their strength by the gold and silver piling up in the royal treasury. Wealth is a hoard, and the surest way to grow yours is to make sure more of it flows <em>in</em>, from selling to foreigners, than flows <em>out</em>, from buying from them. Trade is therefore not a friendly exchange; it is a contest you win at your rivals' expense, and the navy exists in no small part to protect the ships that carry the winnings home.

#### 48 p
To keep the gold flowing the right way, the home country runs the system deliberately. Its colonies are not partners but instruments: they ship raw materials home (cotton, sugar, ore) and are <em>forbidden</em> to manufacture the finished goods themselves, so they must buy those finished goods back from the mother country at a markup. Laws like England's Navigation Acts force that trade to travel only on the home country's own ships. And much of it is run by <strong>chartered companies</strong>, the East India Company the most famous: private firms granted a royal monopoly over a trade route or region, half profit-making business and half arm of the state, sailing with the crown's blessing and sometimes its cannon. That is the world: the state steering commerce, the finished factory kept at home, the raw stuff and the captive markets kept abroad, all of it tuned so the treasury fills.

#### 49 p
Now the definition, which will feel obvious once you've seen the world it came from. <strong>Mercantilism</strong> was Europe's dominant economic doctrine from roughly the 16th to the 18th centuries, before classical liberalism displaced it. A nation's wealth was measured by the <em>bullion</em>, the precious metal, it could accumulate, and the road to power was to maximize exports, minimize imports, and run a trade surplus against rivals. One subtlety the sharper mercantilists grasped: you don't just lock the gold in a vault. Money has to <em>move</em> to generate trade and activity. The aim was accumulation <em>and</em> circulation, both in the service of state power.

#### 50 p
Three features carry over into everything that follows. First, the <strong>state sits above the market</strong>: the economy is an instrument of national power, steered to strengthen the nation against its rivals. Second, you protect your finished industries and export the finished goods: in the old form that meant <strong>high tariffs on manufactured imports</strong>; in the modern, post-tariff form, &ldquo;neomercantilism,&rdquo; it means <strong>non-tariff barriers</strong> like export controls, licensing, standards, and security designations. Third, the nation works through a <strong>stable of chartered champions</strong>: plural firms, competing among themselves, all serving the same national balance of trade. Not one monopoly; a managed team, with the state standing over it.

#### 51 div class=an-label
Why this old idea, and not a newer one

#### 52 p
I reach for mercantilism rather than, say, &ldquo;regulation&rdquo; or &ldquo;protectionism&rdquo; because it captures something those words miss: the reclassification of a thing from <em>commodity to be traded</em> into <em>strategic resource to be governed.</em> That reclassification is the event. Everything downstream (the walls, the champions, the bullion) follows from it.

#### 71 div class=chapter-label
Mechanism 1

#### 72 h2
Intelligence is a utility, not a product <br><span class="chapter-question">&ldquo;Why the cap feels like a meter, not a muzzle&rdquo;</span>

#### 73 div class=card-label
The thesis

#### 74 div class=card-text
Once you see tokens as a <em>utility</em> and data centers as dark factories for intelligence, tiered access stops looking like oppression and starts looking like infrastructure. We tier every utility: bandwidth, fuel octane, industrial versus residential power. The cap on a frontier model is a meter, and meters are normal.

#### 75 div class=card-label
The risk in the framing

#### 76 div class=card-text
Calling it a utility normalizes it, which is precisely what makes it powerful and what should make you wary. The same frame that defuses the &ldquo;censorship&rdquo; panic also hands the state the high ground of &ldquo;critical-infrastructure licensing,&rdquo; a domain where it usually wins.

#### 77 h4
Why &ldquo;censorship&rdquo; is the wrong word

#### 78 p
My first instinct that morning was the word &ldquo;censorship.&rdquo; I think that instinct is wrong, and being precise about why is the first move of the whole analysis. Censorship is the suppression of <em>speech</em>, of something someone wants to express. Nobody told Anthropic what its models may <em>say</em>. The directive restricted <em>who may operate a capability.</em> That is a different category: it is the logic of arms control and export licensing, the same logic that governs centrifuges, certain lab equipment, and, the precedent that haunts this entire booklet, strong cryptography in the 1990s.

#### 79 p
Why insist on the distinction? Because the labels carry their own moral physics. Society has a strong, settled consensus <em>against</em> censorship, and a strong, settled consensus that you <em>can't</em> freely buy a centrifuge. By moving the question from &ldquo;free speech&rdquo; to &ldquo;dual-use capability,&rdquo; the state relocates the fight onto terrain where it ordinarily prevails. So the more accurate word for what you're feeling is not censorship but <strong>rationing</strong>: a cap on how good a tool an ordinary person is permitted to operate. And rationing is something societies do constantly without anyone reaching for the language of tyranny.

#### 80 h4
The utility ladder

#### 81 p
Think of how electricity matured. It did not stay a product that Edison and Westinghouse fought over in the open market, and it was not nationalized. It became a <strong>licensed common carrier</strong>: a regulated utility, overseen by public commissions, with price controls and universal-service obligations. You cannot run a power plant, a bank, or a nuclear reactor without a charter that a regulator can suspend. Read the June 12 kill switch through that lens and it is neither censorship nor even quite arms control but the first assertion that frontier intelligence belongs in the category of <em>critical infrastructure you need a license to operate</em>, and that the license can be pulled in an afternoon.

#### 82 p
That is the ladder this booklet keeps climbing: <em>censorship → rationing → arms control → licensed critical infrastructure.</em> Each rung is a more accurate, and more consequential, description of the same event. The top rung is the one that should hold your attention, because licensing is durable in a way that outrage is not.

#### 114 h4
The AC/DC lesson, and its disanalogy

#### 115 p
There's a tempting argument that no cap can hold: ban the better technology and someone simply reinvents it. The history of electricity seems to prove it: Edison pushed direct current (DC), but alternating current (AC) won because it could be transmitted over distance, and once the idea was understood, anyone could build a transformer. Apply that to AI and you'd conclude the cap is futile: open weights are the &ldquo;someone reinvents AC&rdquo; event, and the ban breaks.

#### 116 p
But there's a disanalogy, and it's the whole ballgame. AC's barrier was <strong>knowledge</strong>: trivial to copy once known. Frontier AI's barrier is <strong>capital and energy</strong>: a gigawatt of power and years of construction. You cannot reinvent a frontier training run in a garage. That is what makes the cap potentially <em>more</em> durable than the historical analogy suggests: the means of production is physically scarce and physically controllable. The chip export controls the US already maintains are the early form of this; the natural extension is from &ldquo;which chips may go to which country&rdquo; to &ldquo;which <em>concentrations</em> may be built at all.&rdquo; <span class="tag tag-speculation">interpretation</span>

#### 117 h4
The acid: efficiency, and the variable that fights it

#### 118 p
The disanalogy has a counter-force. If frontier-class capability keeps getting dramatically cheaper (and recent history says it does), then the capital barrier erodes and the bullion stops being rare. So the durability of the wall is genuinely a <em>race</em>: capital moat versus efficiency curve.

#### 119 p
Here is the variable that tilts the race back toward the wall: <strong>recursive self-improvement</strong>. As models increasingly do the work of AI research (generating training data, running experiments, writing the code of the next system), the leader's rate of progress can accelerate. The key distinction: <em>leakage acts on levels; self-improvement acts on the derivative.</em> A follower who steals your current capability level still cannot catch a trajectory that is itself speeding up. The follower copies where you <em>were</em>; the leader is busy widening where it's <em>going.</em> And because self-improvement runs on compute, it folds straight back into this mechanism's chokepoint: whoever holds the bullion also holds the engine that pulls away. <span class="tag tag-speculation">interpretation</span>

#### 120 p
Said honestly, though, the follower's derivative is not fixed either. The same efficiency gains and published methods that erode the wall also speed the chaser, so the leader pulls away only for as long as the improvement loop stays compute-bound. That is an assumption, not a law, and this whole argument leans on it.

#### 121 p
(A caveat worth keeping honest: self-improvement loops that work tend to be the ones with a hard correctness check, a verifiable reward, rather than a model freely grading its own homework, which degenerates. The strongest versions of this argument assume the verifiable-reward kind. I find that assumption plausible, not certain.)

Mechanism 3

#### 136 h2
Distribute vs. hoard: who holds the dial <br><span class="chapter-question">&ldquo;The market wanted to sell it to everyone. The state said no.&rdquo;</span>

#### 137 div class=card-label
The thesis

#### 138 div class=card-text
There is a single axis under everything, <strong>distribute ↔ hoard</strong>, and the live question is who holds the dial. The market's hand pushes toward distribution (revenue, competition). The state's hand pushes toward hoarding (security, advantage). On June 12, for the first time, the state's hand won.

#### 139 div class=card-label
The hinge

#### 140 div class=card-text
Hoarding a transformative model is only rational under a decisive lead <em>and</em> the ability to consume its capacity. A single firm can't self-consume AGI's output, so the market forces it to sell. A <em>nation</em> can. That's why hoarding economics work at the level of the state, not the lab.

#### 148 h4
Why the market pushes one way and the state the other

#### 149 p
The market's instinct is to distribute, and not out of generosity. The roughly $700B of capex has to be repaid, and you repay it by selling to everyone. Competition reinforces this: in an oligopoly, withhold your best model and a rival rents theirs out and takes the market. And there's a deeper reason a lab <em>can't</em> hoard even if it wanted to: it cannot consume its own capacity. To capture the value of a model that can do any economically useful task, you would have to vertically integrate into <em>every</em> market the model can address: law, drugs, logistics, code, all of it. Impossible. So even a pure profit-maximizer sells the shovels, because it cannot dig all the gold itself.

#### 150 p
The state's instinct runs the other way: a decisive capability is a national advantage, and you don't license a national advantage to foreigners. So the two hands pull in opposite directions, and the interesting thing about June 12 is which one won. <strong>The market wanted to sell Mythos to the entire world. The state said no.</strong> This wasn't capitalism hoarding intelligence; capitalism was straining to distribute it. It was politics overriding the market's distribute-instinct. That is the single cleanest way I can state what happened.

#### 151 h4
Where hoarding's economics actually close: the nation

#### 152 p
The objection to any &ldquo;they'll hoard the AGI&rdquo; story is the one above: no single firm can consume the capacity, so the market forces a sale. But raise the altitude from firm to <em>nation</em> and the objection dissolves. A lab can't run wet labs and law firms and factories; a national economy contains firms that do all of those. So a directive of the form &ldquo;take this capability and go win global markets with it,&rdquo; addressed to a country's champions, clears the economic bar that a single firm fails. This is simply <strong>industrial policy</strong>: channel a strategic advantage to national champions, deny it to rivals. Japan's MITI did a version of it; China's direction of subsidized champions into EVs and solar is a current version. <span class="tag tag-speculation">interpretation</span>

#### 153 h4
Export the goods, not the factory

#### 154 p
But to win a foreign market, your champions must <em>operate</em> in it, which sounds like the very export the wall forbids. The resolution is the oldest mercantilist move there is, and it closes the loop on the &ldquo;money must move&rdquo; principle: <strong>you export the finished goods, not the factory.</strong> The dark factory for intelligence stays home, like a power plant; what crosses the border is the aluminium smelted with the cheap power: the drug, the design, the software product, the intelligence-<em>intensive</em> output. You never ship the grid.

#### 155 p
And this is exactly the shape of the June 12 directive. It restricts foreign <em>nationals operating the model</em> (that's exporting the factory) while saying nothing about foreign <em>customers buying its products</em> (that's just trade). The scope that looked like a clumsy enforcement artifact is, read this way, the policy stated with precision. <span class="tag tag-speculation">interpretation</span>

#### 156 div class=an-label
Two analogies that earn their place

#### 157 p
<strong>Lockheed.</strong> Nobody finds it strange that you can't buy an F-35 on the consumer market. Defense contractors sell decisive capability to governments, not to everyone. If frontier intelligence is being reclassified the same way, a consumer cap is not dystopian; it's the defense-contractor model arriving in software.

#### 158 p
<strong>The Medallion fund.</strong> The most successful quantitative fund in history is closed to outside money and returns capital to investors, because its edge is worth more kept in-house than rented out. Maximal advantage gets hoarded, not sold. A decisive AGI would be the ultimate Medallion fund, which is why, at the limit, the distribute-instinct loses to the hoard-instinct.

#### 175 div class=chapter-label
Mechanism 4

#### 176 h2
Armed or targeted: the per-industry sort <br><span class="chapter-question">&ldquo;Which side of the factory door is your business on?&rdquo;</span>

#### 177 div class=card-label
The thesis

#### 178 div class=card-text
&ldquo;Export the goods, keep the factory&rdquo; quietly sorts every industry into two futures. The businesses the factory-holder could never become get <strong>armed</strong>: frontier access, business as usual, an AI tax. The businesses whose margin <em>is</em> the factory's output get <strong>targeted</strong>: denied the best model while a champion is pointed at their customers.

#### 179 div class=card-label
The risk in relying on it

#### 180 div class=card-text
The sort is dynamic: what a model can't integrate into today creeps toward feasible as agentic reach grows, so &ldquo;safe&rdquo; means &ldquo;safe for now.&rdquo; And the sovereignty shield that protects the targeted has a half-life set by how fast you bleed when under-armed. In adversarial domains it is less a wall than a fuse.

#### 181 h4
The flip side of the goods/factory split

#### 182 p
&ldquo;Export the goods, keep the factory&rdquo; has a flip side that quietly decides which industries thrive and which bleed. The customers who get <em>armed</em> with frontier access are the ones the factory-holder could never become. The ones who get <em>targeted</em> are the ones it can. Put plainly: <strong>if your business <em>is</em> the kind of good the factory makes, you are not a customer; you are a target.</strong> The test is a single question:

#### 183 p
How much of your margin is the <em>thinking</em>, and how much is the <em>moat around the thinking</em>?

#### 184 p
Take two European firms. <strong>A carmaker, say BMW, gets armed.</strong> Almost none of its margin is the thinking; it's the plant, the supply chain, the brand, the dealer network, the regulatory homologation, none of which a model-holder can acquire by being clever. Nobody out-BMWs BMW with a model. So BMW keeps frontier access, runs business-as-usual, and pays a hefty and probably unavoidable <strong>AI tax</strong> on design and logistics. (Its one soft spot is the <em>software edge</em>, the in-car software and the autonomy stack, exactly where a software-native rival <em>can</em> integrate.) <strong>A European cybersecurity or IT-services firm gets targeted.</strong> Its margin <em>was</em> expert cognitive labour sold by the hour, which is precisely the good the factory produces. Under the lens it may be quietly <em>denied</em> the state-of-the-art model while a domestic champion of the model-holding bloc is pointed straight at its customers. Why would a buyer choose the visibly weaker provider? <span class="tag tag-speculation">interpretation</span>

#### 198 p
The rough sorting: <strong>hard to integrate into → armed</strong>. Automotive and heavy industry, pharma (wet labs, trials, patents), energy and infrastructure, banking <em>infrastructure</em>: value rooted in physical, regulated, capital-heavy moats. <strong>Easy to integrate into → targeted</strong>. IT services, software, consulting, content, and cybersecurity: value that <em>is</em> the cognitive output. Four <strong>modifiers</strong> can pull a targeted firm back toward safety: regulatory licensure plus liability (someone must be legally on the hook), proprietary data or trust the holder can't acquire, a physical last mile, and, most important here, sovereignty or jurisdiction requirements. And the sorting is <em>dynamic</em>: what's infeasible to integrate into today (run a fab, a wet lab) creeps toward feasible as models gain agentic and eventually embodied reach. &ldquo;Safe&rdquo; means &ldquo;safe for now.&rdquo;

#### 199 h4
The shield, and why it's a fuse

#### 200 p
That last modifier, sovereignty, is the targeted firm's great hope, and it deserves a hard look, because it is more fragile than it appears. A government can mandate that a Swedish municipality's server racks, software, and security services come from an EU supplier. That's a shield. But <strong>the shield has a half-life, set by how fast and how badly you're hurt by being under-armed.</strong> Where the harm is slow and diffuse (the EU's tariffs on Chinese electric vehicles, say), the shield can hold for years, even decades; slightly pricier cars hurt no one this afternoon. Where the harm is fast, catastrophic, and legible, the shield is not a wall but a <strong>fuse</strong>. In cybersecurity, being a generation behind doesn't mean a slightly worse product; it means you get <em>breached</em>. A handful of major incidents in a short span and the political economy flips overnight: the same municipality that was ordered to buy local now <em>demands</em> the bloc's firm with the state-of-the-art model. <span class="tag tag-speculation">interpretation</span>

#### 201 p
And in adversarial domains the shield is worse than brittle: it is <strong>self-undermining</strong>. The attacker does not obey export controls; criminals and hostile states get the best models through theft, leaks, or their own bloc. So the wall binds the <em>defender</em> and not the <em>attacker</em>, the same pathology that made the 2013 Wassenaar &ldquo;intrusion software&rdquo; controls catch security researchers instead of intruders. You hand your defender an inferior weapon, the un-walled attacker walks through, and the resulting breach is the very thing that forces the shield open. <strong>The shield manufactures the breach that ends it.</strong>

#### 202 p
When it breaks, it breaks badly: dependency enters <em>through the breach</em>, on the worst possible terms, negotiated from a smoking crater, and crisis-driven dependencies almost never unwind. The cruel irony writes itself: <strong>the domains you would most want to keep sovereign (security, defence, critical infrastructure) are exactly where the shield is most brittle, so they fall first and hardest.</strong> Sovereignty is least achievable precisely where it matters most.

At the <strong>classified top</strong>, the shield holds almost by fiat: an intelligence service will never run its crown-jewel security on a foreign, kill-switchable model, because the foreign dependency is itself the unacceptable risk. But that leaves it in an impossible bind: it can't buy foreign and can't afford to lag against adversaries wielding the state of the art, and the only escape is to <em>build its own</em>. That bind is the sharpest argument in this whole booklet for <strong>bloc-level sovereign capability</strong>. In the <strong>important middle</strong>, the municipalities and hospitals with no national-security mandate but plenty to lose, the fuse blows first. At the <strong>commodity bottom</strong>, there is no shield at all; whoever has the best model wins.

#### 214 p
So the durable moves for a targeted firm, or a targeted nation, come down to two. <strong>Pre-position the dependency deliberately</strong>: negotiate frontier access on your terms <em>before</em> the crisis, not from the crater after it. Or <strong>build sovereign capability at the bloc level</strong>. Neither is something an individual firm can do alone, and neither is something the EU can do today. That is what turns &ldquo;regulate the dependency, not the use&rdquo; from a tidy slogan into a security imperative with a countdown on it. And it rhymes with where this booklet began: the recall that started it all was triggered by a <em>cyber</em> capability, so the founding event and the most exposed European industry are one and the same domain. <span class="tag tag-speculation">interpretation</span>

#### 215 p
Let me make the whole armed-versus-targeted split concrete with the one business I know from the inside: my own.

#### 216 div class=fn-label
From the field · the author's own business

#### 217 p
I am about as close to the factory as anyone gets: I teach and consult on AI itself. So the roughly five-year industry timeline this booklet describes, I seem to be living in about one, and looking back is almost vertiginous. Five years ago this was a calm profession: you spent months building a curriculum, then repeated it across years. Now it is constant flux. What I teach changes month to month; the &ldquo;trainings&rdquo; have quietly become discussion sessions with no fixed curriculum; and what clients increasingly want is my <em>opinion</em>, my read on what matters, not me walking them through some standardised Python package.

#### 218 p
The targeted flank is not a forecast for me; it has already happened. A year ago a real slice of my business was Microsoft Copilot training. It dried up almost entirely once Microsoft poured budget into free education and certifications for the EU enterprise market. Vendors owning their own education predates AI, but AI has accelerated it brutally: Anthropic and Google run their own certification and education tracks I simply can't tap into, and Google's NotebookLM now generates, in seconds, the kind of audio and video learning content that <em>used to be my job</em>. #### 227 div class=chapter-label
Mechanism 5

#### 228 h2
Open is a position, not a principle <br><span class="chapter-question">&ldquo;Will open models save us? Read why that hope is mis-shaped.&rdquo;</span>

#### 229 div class=card-label
The thesis

#### 230 div class=card-text
No open-weights commitment survives a winning position. Openness is instrumental to where you stand (a funnel, a moat-eroder, or a catch-up play), never a terminal value. You open-weight when you're behind; you close when you're ahead. Meta, Mistral, and the Chinese labs are three readings of the same rule.

#### 231 div class=card-label
The consequence

#### 232 div class=card-text
&ldquo;China will save us with open models&rdquo; is mis-shaped hope. The open release is a function of position, not values; at a decisive lead, anyone closes. What open weights <em>can</em> give you is not freedom but <em>un-severability</em>, the one thing a kill switch can't take back.

#### 233 h4
Three labs, one rule

#### 234 p
The naive version of the optimistic case says: even if US frontier capability gets walled, open models, especially from China, will keep the field free. I don't think that survives contact with <em>why</em> anyone open-weights in the first place. Watch the same rule produce three different behaviors:

#### 235 p
<strong>Meta.</strong> A decade of open-frontier evangelism, and then, when the economics of $100B-scale compute bit, its frontier development went closed. The &ldquo;Linux of AI&rdquo; thesis did not survive contact with the cost of the frontier. Openness was abandoned the moment closing served the position better.

#### 236 p
<strong>Mistral.</strong> Around January 2026 the framing shifted: open weights were now described, in effect, as the <em>freemium tier</em> of a commercial offering. <span class="tag tag-reported">reported</span> Not a gift but a funnel: open as a customer-acquisition layer beneath a paid product.

#### 237 p
<strong>China.</strong> The open releases that have done so much to erode Western labs' moat are best read as a catch-up-and-commoditize strategy: open-weight your competitor's complement, build ecosystem dependence, erode the leader's advantage. That logic holds precisely as long as you are behind or at parity.

#### 238 p
Put together, these aren't three philosophies; they're three positions on one curve. The rule: <strong>you are &ldquo;open&rdquo; when being open helps you win, and the day closing helps more, you close.</strong> There are now too many examples to call this cynicism. It's just the gravity of the thing.

#### 239 h4
The readable trigger hidden in the rule

#### 240 p
The rule gives you a free instrument. If openness tracks position, then the day a Chinese lab ships its <em>best</em> model <strong>closed</strong> is the day it believes it has taken a lead. So you don't have to guess whether China has hit the frontier curve: watch for the first un-open-weighted Chinese frontier release and read it as the signal. Openness is the tell of the trailing hand. <span class="tag tag-speculation">interpretation</span>

#### 241 div class=an-label
A darker note on Chinese alignment

#### 242 p
There's a detail people underrate. The same Chinese models that refuse on politically sensitive topics are demonstrating something beyond bias: <em>robust, reliable, weight-level behavioral control</em> over a defined topic set. Strip the politics and that is exactly the alignment competency a hoarding state most wants: models that are controllable, steerable, loyal. The West frames alignment around safety and helpfulness; this is alignment demonstrated as <em>state control.</em> Same muscle, different target, and a soft-power instrument in its own right, which is a whole separate worry. <span class="tag tag-speculation">interpretation</span>

#### 243 h4
What open weights actually buy you

#### 244 p
So is the open-model hope worthless? No, but it has to be re-shaped. Open weights don't guarantee a free frontier; they guarantee something narrower and more useful: <strong>un-severability</strong>. A client of an API can be switched off in an afternoon, as June 12 proved. A client of weights it hosts itself cannot. Open weights are not the substrate of freedom; they're the substrate of a connection no one can cut. That's the real reason they matter, and we'll return to it when we get to what you can actually do.

#### 272 h4
The chartered-company structure

#### 273 p
The mercantilist powers didn't run one national monopoly. They chartered several companies (East India, the Levant Company, the Royal African Company) that competed amongst themselves while all serving the same national balance of trade against rival nations. Firm-level plurality and bloc-level singularity were never in tension; the plurality was the hedge and the spur, the singularity was the wall. Map it straight across: within the US bloc, several labs compete, and the state <em>prefers</em> it that way (a nation doesn't bet its frontier on one company, and competition keeps them sharp); at the wall, the state treats their combined capability and compute as one national arsenal facing a rival's arsenal.

#### 274 h4
The labs as organs of a bloc

#### 275 p
Read this way, the labs stop being rivals-to-the-death and start looking like differentiated organs of a single body. One plays the <em>monastic frontier-holder</em>, organized around the research bet. Another is the <em>export-house</em>: a heavily productized lab whose mass-market machine looks like drift if you want it to be a research monastery, but looks like an <em>asset</em> if you want a champion that can convert capability into won markets. A third is the <em>own-your-own-specie</em> player, vertically integrated into its own compute, with the distribution surface to deploy outputs across an entire economy without ever exposing a weight, and a track record of releasing world-changing capability as a controlled scientific commons rather than a shipped product. I'm holding individual motives agnostic here; the <em>structure</em> is the point, and structurally these are complementary roles in one bloc, not three companies fighting over the same square foot. <span class="tag tag-speculation">interpretation</span>

#### 276 h4
Why June 12 fits perfectly

#### 277 p
This is the reading that finally snaps the recall into focus. It was the state asserting <strong>bloc-level control over a firm-level asset.</strong> Anthropic is one chartered company in the national stable; the government demonstrated it can pull that company's flagship to serve the bloc's position. And the &ldquo;foreign national&rdquo; scope is bloc-logic in its purest form: keep the capability inside the bloc's <em>people</em>, regardless of geography. Even the financing tension resolves at this altitude: a lab whose API economics look shaky on its own can still be <em>nationally</em> worth backstopping, because the relevant ledger is the bloc's balance of trade, not one firm's quarterly P&amp;L. The government-lab entanglements already forming look like the embryo of exactly that backstop-and-control bundle. <span class="tag tag-speculation">interpretation</span>

#### 284 div class=chapter-label
Counterweight

#### 285 h2
The counter-current: human adaptability <br><span class="chapter-question">&ldquo;The variable the wall doesn't account for&rdquo;</span>

#### 286 div class=card-label
The hopeful reading

#### 287 div class=card-text
Intelligence may be the &ldquo;last invention,&rdquo; but adaptability is the human engine, and it's the one capability the frozen-after-training paradigm hasn't captured. Adaptability is both a candidate human moat and a <em>social</em> leak: people route around caps, as they always have.

#### 288 div class=card-label
The honest complication

#### 289 div class=card-text
Adaptation is symmetric. The hoarder adapts its controls too; non-tariff barriers evolve. So it's an arms race between the distributed many and the concentrated few, and history is genuinely split on who wins, and over what timescale.

#### 290 p
Every mechanism so far points one way: toward concentration, walls, and control. I don't want to leave it there, because I don't think it's the whole truth, and the missing piece is the most human one.

#### 291 p
We tend to call intelligence the defining human trait. I'm not sure that's right. The trait that actually carried us through evolution is <strong>adaptability</strong>: the capacity to reconfigure ourselves when the environment changes the rules. And here's a reason that's more than sentiment: today's models are <em>frozen after training.</em> They are brilliant and static. They do not restructure themselves in response to a genuinely novel situation the way an organism does. Adaptability, the live rewiring of strategy under new conditions, is precisely the capability the current paradigm has not captured. If anything is a durable human moat against a walled, hoarded frontier, it is this.

#### 292 p
Adaptability is also a <em>leak the wall-builders don't model.</em> Alongside the technical leaks of Mechanism 2 (weights, talent, algorithms) there is a <strong>social leak</strong>: people route around caps. Every concentrated general-purpose technology in history, the printing press, electricity, the internet, was adapted-around by the people it was meant to exclude. Capability finds the cracks because humans are crack-finding machines.

#### 293 p
But I promised honesty over comfort, so here is the complication that keeps this from being a happy ending. <strong>Adaptation is symmetric.</strong> The hoarder adapts too: its controls evolve, its non-tariff barriers get smarter, its enforcement learns. So the real picture is not &ldquo;human ingenuity inevitably wins&rdquo; but an arms race between the distributed many and the concentrated few, and history does not promise the many win it; sometimes a wall holds for generations, sometimes it's gone in a decade. Optimism here is a bet that decentralized adaptation outruns centralized control. That bet is usually right in the <em>long</em> run. It is frequently wrong in the run you actually have to live through.

#### 294 p
I keep this counter-current in the booklet not to soften the thesis but to complete it. The mercantile reclassification of intelligence is real and it is happening. Human adaptability is the force pushing back. Which one you weight more heavily is, in the end, a temperament, and you should know your own.

#### 296 div class=chapter-label
Synthesis

#### 297 h2
The phenomenon, named <br><span class="chapter-question">&ldquo;So this is what's happening to the genAI world&rdquo;</span>

#### 298 p class=lead
Here is the whole thesis in one breath: <strong>frontier intelligence is being reclassified from a product the market wants to distribute into a strategic national resource the state wants to hoard, and mercantilism is the grammar of that reclassification.</strong>

#### 299 p
Accumulate the scarce input (compute, the bullion). Wall the means of production (export controls and the kill switch, the non-tariff barriers). Charter competing national champions (the labs). Export the finished goods of intelligence while keeping the factory at home. Run a surplus against the rival bloc. Once you hold that grammar, the field stops looking like chaos and starts looking like a game with rules, and the June 12 recall stops being an aberration and becomes the <strong>dress rehearsal</strong>: the AGI-hoarding question run early, in public, on a single narrow capability, so we could all watch how it goes.

#### 300 p
The mechanisms don't sit in separate boxes; they lean on one another. The fact that concentrated compute is the one input that can't leak is exactly what gives the bloc's wall something physical to stand on: a barrier made of gigawatts, not just of law. Pushing the other way, the efficiency that keeps making capability cheaper and the open weights that keep spreading it around are forever trying to dissolve that wall; the only thing that reliably re-seals it is a lab taking a real lead and quietly closing its doors. And underneath all of it runs the slow human counter-current: people route around walls, given enough time. So this is not a machine grinding toward one inevitable end but a set of forces pressing against each other, and which way it tips comes down to speed: whether the wall is poured faster than the ways around it are found.

#### 301 p
Which is why the thing to carry out of here is a <strong>grammar</strong>, not a prediction. Once you read intelligence as a mercantile resource, each new headline turns into a recognisable move: a tariff, a charter, a hoarding of bullion, a champion sent to win a market, a wall breached and a dependency walked through. You stop meeting each event as a fresh surprise and start reading it as a move in a game whose rules you now know. That is the whole of what this booklet offers: not to tell you what will happen, but to let you parse what does.

<span class="bf-k">Wrong if</span> compute is still governed only by who-may-buy-which-chip rules at end of 2027, which would undercut the &ldquo;compute is the bullion&rdquo; claim of Mechanism 2.

#### 317 div class=bet-by
Bet 4 · ongoing test

#### 318 div class=bet-claim
<strong>Restrictions keep targeting operation, not outputs.</strong> Future controls gate <em>operating</em> a frontier model (the factory), not <em>importing the goods it produces</em>: the drugs, software, designs.

#### 319 div class=bet-falsify
<span class="bf-k">Wrong if</span> a government broadly restricts AI-<em>produced</em> goods or services while leaving model operation alone, which would invert the &ldquo;export the goods, keep the factory&rdquo; thesis.

#### 320 div class=bet-by
Bet 5 · the China tell

#### 321 div class=bet-claim
<strong>The first time a leading Chinese lab ships its best model closed</strong>, it coincides with credible claims of frontier parity or a lead, confirming that openness tracks position, not principle.

#### 322 div class=bet-falsify
<span class="bf-k">Wrong if</span> a Chinese lab closes its flagship while still clearly behind the frontier (pure ideology or security, no change of position), which would mean Mechanism 5's &ldquo;open is a position&rdquo; law is too strong.

#### 323 div class=bet-by
Bet 6 · by mid-2028

#### 324 div class=bet-claim
<strong>The efficiency acid keeps biting:</strong> a capability matching today's frontier leader becomes replicable at roughly an order of magnitude lower cost, keeping the cap-versus-efficiency contest live.

#### 325 div class=bet-falsify
<span class="bf-k">Wrong if</span> frontier-class efficiency gains plateau, in which case the wall is more durable than the &ldquo;race&rdquo; framing in Mechanism 2 admits, and the hoarders win more easily than I suggested.

#### 326 h3
The case against this whole booklet

#### 327 p
And here is the bet I'd most want you to weigh: that none of this is a strategy at all. The tidiest explanation of June 12 might simply be <strong>messy politics</strong> (a panicked official, a turf fight, a pre-IPO squeeze, an incompetent over-reach) onto which I have retrofitted an elegant 400-year-old doctrine. Humans are pattern-finding machines, and a coherent story about &ldquo;intelligence becoming statecraft&rdquo; is exactly the kind of false order we love to impose on noise. <strong>Here is how you'd tell the difference:</strong> if the mechanisms keep predicting the dated bets above, the structure is real. If the bets fail and each failure needs a fresh ad-hoc excuse, then this booklet was apophenia with good typography, and you should drop it. I genuinely don't know which it is yet, and that uncertainty is the most honest sentence in here.

#### 343 h4
A closing note, and the stance behind every page

I started writing this the morning a collaborator went behind glass, and I'll end where the disclaimer began: I might be wrong about all of it. The lens could be over-fitted to one dramatic event. The mercantilism of intelligence is real and it is underway. So is the ancient human talent for finding the cracks. Which of those you bet on is, in the end, up to you.
